01 / Why a normal curve?

Many small influences.
One familiar shape.

Repeated length measurements can be a little high or a little low. Small effects from positioning, temperature and reading the instrument may combine. Effects pushing up and down can partly cancel. Many results then cluster near a centre, while extreme combined errors are less common.

A useful model: a symmetric bell-shaped curve can describe this pattern. The normal distribution is a model; real measurement data need not follow it exactly.

Move the centre. Change the spread.

Read the picture.

  • μ sets the centre. A larger mean moves the entire curve to the right. If the variance stays fixed, its shape stays the same.
  • σ² controls the spread. A larger variance makes the curve wider and its peak lower. If the mean stays fixed, the centre stays in place.
  • The total area stays 1. A taller peak does not mean a larger total probability.

Variance is not standard deviation.In X ∼ N(100, 9), the mean is 100, the variance is 9, and the standard deviation is √9 = 3. Length is in cm; variance is in cm².

The dashed vertical marks are μ − σ and μ + σ. The solid vertical mark is the mean μ. Both axes stay fixed while you compare curves.

Probability means area.

For a continuous random variable, P(a < X < b) is the area under the curve between a and b. The height of the curve at x is a density, not P(X = x).

P(X = a) = 0

So P(X < a) = P(X ≤ a), and P(X > a) = P(X ≥ a). A single endpoint has zero area.

Two facts we will use repeatedly.

Area to the left of μ = 0.5
Area to the right of μ = 0.5

The curve is symmetric about μ. Matching regions on opposite sides of the centre have equal areas.

First identify the centre and the spread. Then ask which area you need.

02 / Standardisation

Different measurements.
The same Z curve.

A Z-score tells us how many standard deviations a value is above or below its mean. This lets us use one probability table for every normal distribution.

P(X > 106)

Original units

P(Z > 2)

Standard deviation units

Subtract the mean. Divide by the SD.

Z = (X − μ) / σ
If X ∼ N(μ, σ²), then Z ∼ N(0, 1).
  • A value at the mean has Z = 0.
  • A value above the mean has positive Z.
  • A value below the mean has negative Z.
  • Because σ > 0, the inequality direction stays the same.
Divide by σ, not σ².For N(100, 9), use (106 − 100)/3 = 2. Do not divide by 9.

The shaded probability is unchanged. The horizontal scale is expressed in different units.

The remaining task is to read the standard normal table correctly.
